01 · Overview

What this project is

For my Discovery Project, I decided to learn printed circuit board (PCB) design and manufacturing. Almost every electronic product, from a phone to a racecar, is built around a PCB, so being able to design one is a core skill in electrical engineering today.

To learn it, I designed a real board: a small-scale version of the ready-to-move light on a Formula SAE electric racecar. This is the light that indicates when the car is fully ready and can officially start.

02 · The Problem

The Problem

Before this project I had built plenty of electronics, but always by soldering wires between ready-made modules, as I did on my game controller and my drones. That works for a prototype, but it isn't how real electronics are made. On HyTech Racing's electric racecar, every electrical system lives on a custom PCB, and I couldn't contribute to that work until I knew how to design one.

So the question for this project was simple: can I go from never having designed a circuit board to completing a board that serves a real purpose on a racecar?

03 · The Process

The Process

I learned Altium Designer, the professional PCB design software that HyTech Racing uses. I had two main sources:

  • HyTech's own documentation. The team keeps an internal library of guides, which I worked through to learn its design process and standards.
  • AI as a tutor. I used AI tools to teach me keyboard shortcuts and faster ways of working while I was designing the board, which made the software far less intimidating.

Choosing the ready-to-move light as my first board was deliberate. It is small enough for a beginner to finish, but it is a real part of an FSAE car, so I was learning on a design that matters and not on a made-up exercise.

04 · The Outcome

The Outcome

I completed the design of my small-scale ready-to-move light board in Altium Designer, and I can now take a circuit from an idea to a finished board layout. That was the main goal of the project, and it is a skill I am already using on HyTech's electrical team.

The second half, physically manufacturing the board, is still ahead of me. I plan to learn and work at the HIVE Makerspace, Georgia Tech's electrical makerspace, to build it. I haven't reached that step yet, but I plan to in the near future, and I will add photos of the finished board here.

05 · Reflection

What I learned and what's next

The biggest lesson was that a complicated professional tool becomes manageable when you learn it through a real project. Reading documentation alone would not have stuck. Having a specific board to finish gave every new skill a purpose.

I also learned that design is only half of the job. Knowing both how a board is designed and how it is physically made matters to me, because my goal is to work on electrical systems in the automotive and aviation fields, where hardware has to be built, tested and trusted. This project is my first step toward that, and manufacturing the board is the next one.
